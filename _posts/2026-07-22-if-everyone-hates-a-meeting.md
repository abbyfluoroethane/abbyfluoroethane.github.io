If everyone hates a meeting, it should probably not exist. If nobody likes the time, it should probably be changed. If your meeting is redundant with another — a morning meeting covering the same ground as yesterday's 3PM end-of-day meeting — you should *really* just pick one.

"Hate" isn't the test. Plenty of necessary things are unpleasant. Redundancy is the test: does this meeting do anything the last one didn't? Which brings me to the gemba walk.

## The idea behind gemba is good

For the uninitiated: *gemba* is the place where the work actually happens — the floor, the line, wherever the real thing is being built. Going to gemba means going to see reality with your own eyes instead of trusting the report. Reports flatten things. They lose the detail nobody thought to write down, the problem nobody flagged because it didn't fit a field in Jira. You go to the floor to catch what the paperwork missed. It's a core concept in lean manufacturing for a reason.

## The walk

Every morning I walk the floor, and the walk covers exactly what the EOD meeting covered the day before. *Same topics. Less substance. More time.* All because now we're doing it while ambling around instead of standing still.

A gemba walk that's redundant *every day* isn't a walk. If it's working, it surfaces the stuff the EOD didn't — that's the entire point of going to look. One clean day that turns up nothing is fine, expected even. But every day, in the same shape, read aloud on the move, next to loud machinery? That isn't a scheduling annoyance. It's a diagnosis. It means nobody's actually looking. We're not going to gemba to see reality; we're performing the shape of going to gemba. It's a zombie process — the ritual kept moving after the thing that animated it died.

I'm not anti-process. I'd defend gemba to anyone. That's exactly why the hollow version bothers me! It takes a practice whose whole value is *catching what you'd otherwise miss* and turns it into a walk where, by construction, we catch nothing.

## The worst part

A pointless meeting doesn't cost you the length of the meeting. Thinking about it as "fifteen wasted minutes" is a trap that ignores the externalities.

Because this one moved earlier, I now have to be in the office an hour earlier. Transit doesn't flex around an arbitrary start time — the buses and trains line up how they line up, so getting in earlier means an earlier bus, which slides the whole day. The meeting itself is maybe ten or fifteen minutes of walking in a circle. The actual cost is closer to two hours, spent entirely outside the meeting, absorbed by the gravity well it creates.

And it isn't only me. Multiply that gravity well by everyone required to be there, every weekday, and the ledger stops looking anything like fifteen minutes.

*That* is the price of a zombie process. Not the time inside it — the way it warps everything it touches. An 8AM ritual that surfaces nothing reaches out through the transit schedule and eats the edges of my morning and my evening, every day, in exchange for information I already had at 3PM yesterday.

If everyone hates a meeting, look closer.

Maybe it's just necessary and unpleasant, and that's fine. But if everyone hates it *and it tells you nothing new* — if it's redundant, day after day, with something you already do — that redundancy is the tell. The meeting becomes an autopsy report. Something already died. Now you're just walking its corpse around the floor every morning.

**Pick one.**
