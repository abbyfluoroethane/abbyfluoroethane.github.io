## *"copyright is fake and gay and shouldn't exist."*

if i hear one more person complain about licenses and content rights i'm going to shoot myself in the street. i'm obviously being *a bit* hyperbolic, but holy shit you people are annoying. and if you ask me, i think you're plain wrong about how the world should work. *surely there is a better way, right?*

### i don't mean copyleft

let me be precise about what i'm arguing, because every time this comes up someone assumes i just want everything to be GPL. no. i am not arguing for the abolition of "restrictive" licenses. i am arguing for the abolition of copyright and legal ownership of information *as a whole*. especially on the internet.

copyleft doesn't go far enough. it can't, structurally.

the GPL is copyright bullet-dodging. it uses the legal machinery of ownership to force openness. *clever!* genuinely one of the great hacks of the 20th century, but it still depends on copyright existing to have any teeth. **a copyleft license is a copyright license.** if you think the GPL is good because information should be free, congratulations, you agree with me *and* you're taking the coward's route there.

this is a fringe opinion. i frequently get into arguments about it. but apparently it's less fringe than i thought: after poking around [my girlfriend's](https://zackerthescar.com) website last year i found out she's a copyright abolitionist too. and my beloved niche internet microcelebrity [they should make larger fruits](https://www.youtube.com/@theyshouldmakelargerfruits) at one point wrote the [Copyright Abolition Manifesto](https://zackerthescar.com/copyright-abolition/copyright-abolition-manifesto/) (now reposted on riley's site), which mirrors my opinions almost point for point. boy it feels good when people on the internet feel the same way you do about things.

### free, as in use

a while back i published the [god i wish that were me public license](https://github.com/abbyfluoroethane/GIWTWM-PL). the joke explains itself if you know, and if you don't, i'm not explaining it. but underneath the bit is my actual position: everything i put on the internet is there to be used. copied, forked, sampled, trained on, whatever. i put it there. that was the point.

which brings me to the argument that actually makes people mad: **i don't care that AI models train on freely available data, and i don't think you should either.** or more precisely — i think you have to pick a lane. either you believe in open access, in which case openness doesn't come with an asterisk that says "except for uses i find distasteful," or you believe in access control, in which case go all in: DRM, licenses, paywalls, lawyers, the whole apparatus. both are coherent positions. what's incoherent is "information wants to be free (until a computer reads it)."

software is where this gets the most embarrassing. if you published your code publicly, a human reading it, learning from it, and writing something similar was always fine. that's not piracy, that's literally how everyone learns to program. so what's categorically different when the reader is a model? scale? speed? that it makes money for someone you don't like? those are complaints about *who benefits*, not about the act itself. and "copying is fine except when the wrong people do it" isn't principled. it's cowardice!

my arguement extends not just to github repos, but also things like shadow libraries. books3 was a torrent of almost two hundred thousand books nobody published freely. paywalled papers, pirated epubs, scraped everything. if my argument only defended training on stuff people chose to make public, that would be a real hole in it. but it isn't, because i don't believe in the paywall either. my line is that information, once it exists, isn't ownable. the pirated book is exactly as unownable as the public repo. i'm not defending the training companies by accident of scope; i'm telling you the shadow library is good, sci-hub is good, and the fact that it took AI labs torrenting books for anyone to get mad about enclosure of knowledge is kind of funny.

one important carve-out: copying is not plagiarism. if you take my track and repost it under your name, the problem isn't that you copied — it's that you lied. misattribution is fraud, a completely different wrong, and it survives copyright abolition just fine because it was never about property in the first place. attribution is a social norm, and social norms are enforced the way they've always been: by everyone thinking you're a dick if you break them. i want the fences gone. i still want your name on your work. these are not in tension.

to be clear, i'm not saying you have to *like* it. you can think the companies doing the training are ghouls. i'm saying your license was always a request backed by state violence, and if your position on information freedom flips based on who's doing the reading, you never had a position.

### libertarian, as in non-aggression

my politics are classical libertarian, leaning libertarian socialist, and the closest thing i have to a moral axiom is the non-aggression principle. summarized for the people in the back: don't be a dick. don't initiate force against people or their stuff.

so here's the thing about copying. if i copy your file, you still have your file. nothing was taken. nothing was damaged. no aggression occurred, by definition, because copying is non-rivalrous. "theft" of information is a metaphor, and it's a bad one, because actual theft deprives you of the thing. copying deprives you of nothing except a hypothetical sale you were never guaranteed and a feeling of control you never really had.

now look at the other side of the ledger. what does *enforcing* copyright require? lawsuits. raids. seizing servers. putting people in prison for sharing files. a legal apparatus that will absolutely ruin your life over a folder of mp3s. one of these two activities involves men with guns showing up at your door, and it's not the copying. the aggression in the copyright equation is entirely on the enforcement side. if you take the non-aggression principle seriously — actually seriously, not just when it's convenient for property maximalism — copyright doesn't survive contact with it.

and yeah, i hear the objection loading: "but if anyone can copy anything, how do you compete?" the same way anyone competes. i'm an unapologetic free market believer, and i think the market for information should work like the market for everything else. **execution wins.** if someone takes your idea and does it better, cheaper, or faster than you — literally just get good. your moat was never the idea. it's your speed, your taste, your reputation, the community that trusts you, the fact that you're the one who actually ships. ideas are the cheapest commodity on earth. everyone has them. almost nobody does the thing. competition reigns king, baybeeeeee.

### oh, but would you think of the artists?!

this a load-bearing objection, and it deserves a load-bearing answer: "copyright is how creators get paid. abolish it and you starve the people who make the things you love." fair. if my position meant no one could make art for a living, that would be a serious problem.

but let's look at how artists actually get paid *right now*, under the system we're allegedly defending. 

spotify pays fractions of a cent per stream, and most of that goes to the label anyway. the majority of working musicians make their money from shows, merch, commissions, bandcamp drops, patreon, sync work, teaching. this is all direct exchange and patronage, not royalties on copies. copyright revenue for the median artist rounds to zero. the entities copyright actually protects are labels, publishers, and rightsholder aggregators. companies whose business model is owning other people's work. copyright as it exists is the corporate asset owning class wearing an artist costume. defending it "for the artists" is defending disney with extra steps.

and the models that DO pay artists — patronage, live performance, commissions, merch, community support — all survive copyright abolition completely intact, because none of them depend on controlling copies. they depend on people wanting to support you, which is a much older and much more durable technology than intellectual property law.

would some things change under abolition? yes, and i'm not going to pretend otherwise. 

the transition is genuinely harder for some fields than others. i make music, and music has moats copyright never provided. a novelist or an illustrator has a rougher road: their work *is* the copy, and patronage for a midlist author is shakier than for a musician with a scene behind her. i think those fields adapt too with serialization, crowdfunding, and direct commission. y'know, the same patronage machinery that fed writers for centuries before the statute of anne, but i won't tell you the adjustment is free. some business models die. that's not the same as art dying. the art survives; some middlemen don't.

### do whatever the fuck you want

here's the thing the pro-copyright cohort always misses: we already know what a world without permission looks like, because the best parts of this one were built there.

open source runs the internet. the server this post lives on, the browser you're reading it in, the toolchain that compiled both were built in the open, by people who let anyone copy their work, and it didn't collapse into a tragedy of the commons. it became the commons. the single largest collaborative engineering effort in human history happened *because* nobody could fence anything off, not despite it.

and it's not just software. entire genres of music exist because nobody asked permission. hip hop was built on unlicensed breaks. jungle exists because of one drum loop everyone "stole." internet music culture — edits, flips, dariacore, the nightcore-to-whatever pipeline — is a permission-free zone producing some of the most alive art anywhere, and every bit of it is legally radioactive. the law says this shouldn't exist. ***it exists anyway, and it rules.*** at some point you have to ask whether the law is describing anything real.

my vision for a copyright free world isn't utopian. it's just a maximalist take on non aggression and the freedom of art and information. memes already work this way. folklore always worked this way. every joke you've ever retold, every recipe your family adapted, every riff a guitarist learned off a record: unlicensed derivatives, all the way down. copyright is a 300-year blip in a hundred-thousand-year remix.

so: do whatever the fuck you want. copy my stuff. flip it, fork it, train on it, make it better and embarrass me. *i double-dog dare you.*

the worst case is you make something bad and nobody cares. the best case is you make something good, and then there's more good stuff, and that's the entire point of any of this.
